+# Chapter 1 — The Lantern at Milepost 9
+
+Mara Venn knew the sound of a healthy rail by the way rain sang on it.
+
+On most nights the northbound line gave back a thin, even note under her hammer, bright as a spoon against porcelain. Tonight, beneath the storm and the sea wind, the rail answered dull. Not broken. Not yet. But tired in the metal, as if something heavy had passed that was not on the schedule.
+
+She crouched with her lantern hooded against the rain and ran two gloved fingers along the inner edge of the track. Grit collected under the nail of her forefinger: salt, rust, a thread of black weed dragged up from the tidal marsh. The weed had no business here. The nearest breach in the sea wall lay half a mile west, and the ballast between was clean when she inspected it at dusk.
+
+Mara wiped her hand on her coat and marked the sleeper with chalk.
+
+“Seven east of Milepost Eight,” she said into the field recorder clipped inside her collar. “Possible drag contact. No visible fracture. Recheck after downpour eases.”
+
+The recorder clicked softly, dutiful and unimpressed.
+
+Above her, the town of Bellwether hunched behind its harbor lamps. The houses were stacked on the cliff like damp ledgers, all shuttered windows and smoking chimneys. At low tide the place smelled of kelp and coal smoke. At high tide, as now, the sea climbed the pilings and breathed through the alleys until even the post office seemed afloat. People said the railway kept Bellwether attached to the rest of the coast. Mara had always thought the railway kept the sea from learning how alone the town was.
+
+She stood, shoulders aching under the oilskin, and checked her watch. 01:43. The midnight freight had cleared the south switch eleven minutes late. No passenger traffic until dawn. She had four miles left before she could return to the depot stove, hang her coat over a chair, and pretend the cold had not reached her bones.
+
+Then she saw the light.
+
+It burned ahead of her where the track bent inland, a steady amber point low beside the line. For a moment Mara assumed it was a signal lamp blown askew, but the nearest signal mast stood behind her, dark and accounted for. The next was at the bridge, beyond Milepost Ten.
+
+She lifted her own lantern and narrowed the shutter. The strange light did not flicker in answer. It burned with the stubborn, sheltered glow of a flame behind clean glass.
+
+Mara did not hurry. Hurrying on wet ballast broke ankles and ruined reports. She moved sleeper to sleeper, boots finding the familiar rhythm, hammer in her right hand and the recorder now open beneath her chin.
+
+“Unidentified light visible near Milepost Nine,” she said. “No crew expected. Investigating.”
+
+Wind shoved rain across the track in pale sheets. The amber point vanished, returned, vanished again. When Mara reached the bend, the old milepost rose out of the scrub on the seaward side, black with rot and nearly hidden by bracken.
+
+Milepost 9 had been abandoned twenty-two years ago.
+
+Not officially removed, because railway paperwork was a form of geology, but made irrelevant when the line was realigned after the cliff fall. The true milepost stood farther inland now, a square concrete marker with reflective paint and a metal plate. This old one belonged to a section of track that no longer existed. Mara had seen it often enough in daylight: a leaning timber beside a shallow ditch, its number carved deep and filled with lichen.
+
+Tonight a lantern hung from its iron peg.
+
+It was not a modern inspection lamp. The frame was brass, greened with age, and the glass chimney was uncracked. No rain touched the flame. Water struck the lantern, streamed around it, and fell away in silver strings, but the amber core burned dry and still.
+
+Mara stopped three sleepers short.
+
+Competence had rules. First, name the ordinary possibilities. A prank by boys from the cannery. A stranded walker. A heritage collector with poor timing and worse sense. Second, protect the line. Third, touch nothing until she had observed enough to explain why touching had been necessary.
+
+She swept her own light across the ground.
+
+No footprints marked the mud around the post. Not even hers, though she stood close enough that the ditch should have taken the print of her left boot. The bracken bent under the rain, undisturbed. The rusted peg had no chain, yet the lantern hung there as if fastened by weight alone.
+
+Mara swallowed. The storm filled the pause for her.
+
+“Lantern present on disused Milepost Nine,” she recorded. Her voice came out flatter than she felt. “No visible approach tracks. Object appears dry despite weather.”
+
+A sound came from the lantern.
+
+Not a crackle. Not the hiss of oil. A wheel sound: iron rims turning slowly over joined rail. Mara’s body knew it before her mind named it. The rhythm was wrong for any train on the active line. Too slow, too close, and coming from the empty ditch where the old trackbed had been torn up before she was born.
+
+She raised her hammer.
+
+The lantern flame leaned toward her, though the wind blew hard the other way. In its glass she saw a reflection that did not match the night behind her: twin rails shining under moonlight, a semaphore arm lowered, and the black front of an engine waiting without steam.
+
+Then the recorder at her collar clicked on by itself.
+
+A man’s voice, thin with distance and static, whispered through the speaker.
+
+“Inspector Venn,” it said. “Do not clear the line.”
+
+Mara held still until the whisper died. Rain ran down the back of her neck. Far inland, the dawn passenger’s signal remained dark, as it should.
+
+She reached for her radio, eyes fixed on the impossible lantern, and called the depot.
