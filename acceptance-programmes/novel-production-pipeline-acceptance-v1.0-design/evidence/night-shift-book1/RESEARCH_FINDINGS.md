## Research Findings

### RES-01: Lorazepam for seizure management — CORRECT with caveat

**Manuscript claim:** Mara orders lorazepam 2mg IV for Mrs. Park's seizure lasting >5 minutes. Liam draws it up. Mara administers it IV push, slow.

**Sourced fact:** Lorazepam is a benzodiazepine used for status epilepticus. IV onset 1-5 minutes. Standard IV dose for seizures is 0.1 mg/kg (typically 4mg for an average adult). The manuscript's 2mg dose is within the lower therapeutic range but slightly low for a 71-year-old post-stroke patient. (Source: Wikipedia/Lorazepam, Hospital Technical Reference)

**Source interpretation:** The Hospital Technical Reference notes: "Midazolam IV or IM is often preferred in Australian hospitals for rapid termination. Lorazepam may be second-line." The reference also states: "Lorazepam may not be stocked on all general wards" — it's marked [UNCERTAIN — hospital-specific].

**Uncertainty:** The choice of lorazepam over midazolam may be less authentic for an Australian hospital setting. However, lorazepam is a valid choice and the novel is set in a specific fictional hospital where the drug protocol may differ. The 2mg dose is low but defensible (lower dose for an elderly post-stroke patient, cautious administration).

**Manuscript implication:** The medical scene is dramatized correctly — the protocol (time the seizure, protect airway, turn on side, draw up drug, check cannula, administer IV push slow) is accurate. The student drawing up but not administering (scope of practice) is correct. The timing (5 minutes = status epilepticus threshold, >5 minutes needs benzo) is correct per current definition.

**Verdict:** CORRECT with minor caveat. Lorazepam is a valid choice but midazolam may be more authentic for Australian practice. This is a medical accuracy nuance, not an error. No prose change required unless Andrew wants to swap to midazolam for greater Australian authenticity.

### RES-02: Status epilepticus definition — CORRECT

**Manuscript claim:** "Under five minutes was a seizure. Over five minutes was status epilepticus."

**Sourced fact:** "Convulsive status epilepticus... lasting at least 5 minutes without return to normal." (Source: Wikipedia/Status epilepticus)

**Verdict:** CORRECT. The 5-minute threshold is the current standard definition.

The timeline (seizure started, Mara ran to get help, came back, drew up drug, administered) taking approximately 4-7 minutes is realistic. IV lorazepam onset is 1-5 minutes, so the seizure stopping shortly after administration is plausible.

### RES-04: Terminal surge (Aunty Dot's last good night) — CORRECT

**Manuscript claim:** Aunty Dot, who had been declining, suddenly sits up, eats a sandwich, is fully alert and conversational, then dies at 05:12. Mara recognizes this as "the surge."

**Sourced fact:** Terminal lucidity (also called "the surge" or "terminal rally") is a documented phenomenon where patients near death experience a period of clarity, energy, and appetite shortly before death. It is well-known in nursing and palliative care.

**Verdict:** CORRECT. The portrayal is authentic and medically accurate. The timing (hours before death, followed by gradual decline and cardiac cessation) is consistent with documented terminal surge patterns.

### RES-05: Death verification and post-mortem procedures — CORRECT

**Manuscript claim:** After Aunty Dot's death, Mara sits with the body, then the doctor verifies, family is notified, and post-mortem care is completed. Mara charts: "Patient Dorothy Anderson, RN (retired), deceased at 05:12."

**Sourced fact:** Per the Hospital Technical Reference: "Verification of death — nurses perform this. Certification of death — a medical practitioner must complete." The sequence (nurse verifies, doctor certifies, family notified, body prepared) is correct. (Source: Hospital Technical Reference, Section 3)

**Note:** The chart says "RN (retired)" but the manuscript doesn't establish that Aunty Dot was a retired nurse — she was a cleaner's daughter and a cleaner herself, not a nurse. This is a minor error: Aunty Dot was not a nurse. However, "RN (retired)" could refer to the patient's former occupation if she had been a nurse, but she wasn't — she was a cleaner at a school. This is likely a GLM-5.2 generation error.

**Verdict:** Procedures CORRECT. The "RN (retired)" notation appears to be an ERROR — Aunty Dot was not a nurse. Flag for revision.

### RES-06: Student nurse scope of practice — CORRECT

**Manuscript claim:** Liam (final-year student) draws up lorazepam but says "I can't administer it. I'm a student." Mara administers it. Liam does the cannula check, seizure timing, and patient positioning.

**Sourced fact:** Per Hospital Technical Reference: "Students cannot administer medications unsupervised (except under direct RN supervision in some later-year placements)." Final-year students may administer medications with RN co-signing. Drawing up medications is within student scope.

**Verdict:** CORRECT. The student drawing up but not administering is authentic. The RN administering is correct.

### RES-09: Sundowning — CORRECT

**Manuscript claim:** Bed 8, Mr. Papadopoulos, sundowning. "He'll be agitated between 20:00 and 22:00. Don't argue with him. He thinks he's at a hotel."

**Sourced fact:** Sundowning is increased confusion and agitation in the late afternoon/evening in patients with dementia or delirium. It is a well-documented phenomenon in hospitalized elderly patients.

**Verdict:** CORRECT.

### RES-10: Oxygen delivery — CORRECT

### RES-11: Post-stroke seizure — CORRECT

**Manuscript claim:** Mrs. Park (71, post-stroke) has a seizure with no prior seizure history. Mara says "seizures can happen after a stroke."

**Sourced fact:** Post-stroke seizures are a well-documented complication. They can occur days to weeks after the stroke, particularly with left-hemisphere infarcts. The first seizure may be the first symptom.

**Verdict:** CORRECT.

### RES-12: "Obs" terminology — CORRECT

**Manuscript claim:** Throughout the novel, "obs" is used for observations/vital signs. "Obs machine" for the portable vital signs monitor.

**Sourced fact:** "Obs" is standard Australian nursing shorthand for observations/vital signs. The portable vital signs machine is commonly called "the obs machine" or "the observation machine" in Australian hospitals.

**Verdict:** CORRECT.
