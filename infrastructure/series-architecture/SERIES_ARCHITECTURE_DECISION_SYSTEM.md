## PURPOSE

The Decision System provides a structured process for making architecture decisions. It does not make decisions for the author. It ensures decisions are explicit, informed, documented, and reviewable.

---

## DECISION TYPES

### TYPE A: FOUNDATIONAL — before Book 1
All structural axis declarations, narrative economy identification, reader promise, natural ending condition. Recorded in the Contract.

### TYPE B: STRUCTURAL — during the series, at review points
Continuity tier changes, cast rotation, escalation shifts, architecture transitions, phase appendix creation.

### TYPE C: CREATIVE OVERRIDES — when judgement conflicts with architecture
Spending a protected resource early, changing the reader promise, extending past the natural ending, any decision contradicting the Contract.

---

## DECISION PROCESS

### STEP 1: FRAME THE DECISION
State as a question. "Should the overarching mystery be partially revealed in Book 3?" Not: "What should happen in Book 3?"

### STEP 2: IDENTIFY WHAT'S AFFECTED
Which axes? Which economy elements? Which longevity principles? Which failure modes?

### STEP 3: ASSESS CONSEQUENCES
For each option:

- **GENERATES** — Does this create new story capacity? Renewable or finite?
- **CONSUMES** — Does this spend resources? Which ones? Protected?
- **PROTECTS** — Does this preserve resources for later? Cost of waiting?
- **RISKS** — Which failure modes does this expose? How likely? How severe?
- **CLOSES** — Does this close off future possibilities the series needs?

### STEP 4: CHECK AGAINST THE CONTRACT
Does this conflict with any provision? If no: proceed. If yes: Type C override.

### STEP 5: RECORD
1. Decision
2. Rationale
3. Alternatives considered
4. Consequences accepted
5. Date

### STEP 6: UPDATE THE CONTRACT
If the decision changes the architecture, update the Contract and note the change in the Review Log.

## KEY DIAGNOSTIC QUESTIONS

### Q1: "Should this series be open-ended or bounded?"
- Is there a renewable engine? If not, bounded.
- Does the core question have a definitive answer? If yes, bounded.
- Does the protagonist have a growth vector with a natural endpoint? If yes, bounded.
- Does the setting generate new incidents indefinitely? If yes, can be open-ended.

**Warning:** Open-ended without a renewable engine is the most common longevity error.

### Q2: "Should I reveal [major element] in this book?"
- Is this a protected resource? Check the Contract.
- If revealed, what drives the remaining books?
- Does the reveal generate new questions, or close the mystery?
- Is the reveal driven by story need or by desire for a dramatic moment?

### Q3: "Should the protagonist change permanently?"
- What is the Contract's character permanence setting?
- Does the change create new story capacity or close it?
- Does the change honour the reader promise?
- Engine-Permanence Compatibility Check: does the engine require the protagonist to remain functionally unchanged? If yes, Character Permanence (Axis 4) must be Static or Accreting, not Evolving or Transformative.

### Q4: "Should I bring in a new protagonist?"
- Is the current protagonist exhausted (growth vector complete, no new material)?
- Does the new protagonist bring new generators?
- Does the shift honour the reader promise?
- Is this a planned rotation or a reactive replacement?

### Q5: "Should I extend the series past its natural ending?"
- Has the ending condition been met? Check the Contract.
- Has the series found a genuinely new engine, or is it running on momentum?
- Does the extension honour the reader promise?
- Is the extension driven by story need or external pressure?

### Q6: "Should I change the continuity tier?"
- Is the series drifting? Is the drift intentional?
- Does the higher tier serve the story or constrain it?
- What's the cost to new readers?

### Q7: "Should I change the escalation mode?"
- Has the current mode reached its limit?
- Is the new mode compatible with the reader promise?
- Does it generate new capacity?

### Q8: "Is this book consuming too much?"
- What does this book spend? What does it generate?
- Is it running a generation-consume deficit?
- If yes, is it justified? What will drive the next book?

### Q9: "Should I transition the series architecture?" (Salvage from Candidate A)
- Is the transition deliberate or accidental drift?
- What trigger necessitates the change?
- What is the reader-promise impact?
- Which safeguards still apply? Which need replacing?
- What new risks does the new architecture introduce?
- Is a phase appendix needed?

### Q10: "Should this be a successor series rather than an extension?"
- Has the original series reached its natural ending?
- Is the new material genuinely different (new protagonist, new phase, new engine)?
- Would a new Contract serve this better than extending the old one?
- Can the reader promise carry forward in adapted form?

## OVERRIDE PROTOCOL

1. **Identify the provision overridden** — which Contract section, which principle
2. **State the creative reason** — why is the override better for the story?
3. **Identify the trade-off** — what structural risk does the override create?
4. **Assess the consequence** — what might happen?
5. **Decide whether to accept the risk** — is the creative gain worth it?
6. **Document** in the Contract's Creative Overrides section
7. **Monitor** for the consequences the override risks

---

## REVIEW TRIGGERS

1. After Book 1
2. Before each subsequent book
3. When a structural problem is detected
4. When a creative opportunity conflicts with the architecture
5. When the series approaches its natural ending
6. When the series has passed its natural ending
7. When the author feels the series becoming repetitive
8. When reader feedback indicates drift
9. When a planned architecture transition is approaching (Salvage from Candidate A)
10. When canon accumulation is felt as a burden
