## 1. PURPOSE

This framework provides a structured pre-production process for fiction series design. It answers the structural and longevity questions that determine whether a series can sustain itself across multiple books — before Book 1 is planned.

It is an **upstream gate**, sitting before:
1. Series Bible finalisation
2. Showrunner architecture
3. Book 1 outline
4. Writer execution

It does not replace creative judgement. It makes structural decisions visible so they can be made consciously.

**Integration warning:** Do not treat this framework as Novel Production System doctrine until it has been tested against at least two real Arden series and approved by Andrew. The framework is Working Storycraft / Domain Knowledge, not institutional policy.

## 2. CORE DESIGN PHILOSOPHY

### 2.1 Narrative Economy

Every series runs on a **narrative economy** — resources that generate, consume, and preserve story capacity. Longevity is not about having "more story to tell." It's about maintaining the balance between what generates future books and what each book consumes.

A series fails when consumption outpaces generation, or when the things that generate future books are spent too early.

### 2.2 Architecture Serves Story

This framework does not prescribe what a series should be. It asks what a series *is* — structurally — and whether the author has made that decision consciously.

A useful test for every component: *Would completing this contract make the actual series easier and safer to write?* If a field does not materially improve creative judgement, remove it.

### 2.3 Against False Precision

No framework can predict the creative life of a series. Numerical thresholds create an illusion of rigour that storycraft doesn't support. This framework uses:

- **Structural logic** — decisions that follow from the series's nature
- **Heuristic guidance** — patterns from how series succeed or fail, offered as guidance not law
- **Diagnostic questions** — questions that surface blind spots rather than prescribe answers

Each is labelled as such. Where Candidate A offered numerical indices (Canon Burden Index, Escalation Budget percentages, Exhaustion Scores), Candidate C retains the diagnostic value but strips the false precision.

### 2.4 Creative Override

Any guidance may be overridden by creative judgement when:
- The trade-off is understood (the author knows what they're giving up)
- The decision is documented (the reason is recorded)
- The consequences are accepted (the author owns the risk)

## 3. SERIES TAXONOMY

### 3.1 Approach: Axes, Not Boxes

Candidate B's axis model is retained as the core taxonomy. Instead of forcing series into type boxes, the framework identifies **seven structural axes** — fundamental decisions that define a series architecture. A series is described by its position on each axis, not by which box it fits in.

This approach:
- Describes series accurately (a series can be high on one axis and low on another)
- Reveals tensions (episodic surface + evolving characters = a structural tension worth examining)
- Doesn't force false choices
- Makes the architecture visible

### 3.2 The Seven Structural Axes

#### AXIS 1: CLOSURE MODEL
- **Open-ended** — No planned ending. Continues as long as it generates viable books.
- **Bounded** — Natural ending exists, book count flexible. Ends when its core question is answered.
- **Approximate** — Target range (e.g., 5-7 books) with flexibility.
- **Fixed** — Predetermined book count with a planned ending.

#### AXIS 2: CONTINUITY DENSITY
- **Episodic (Tier 1)** — Almost any volume can be read first.
- **Progressive (Tier 2)** — Books resolve individually; relationships/world/character arcs progress.
- **Sequential (Tier 3)** — Books form a continuous narrative. Reading order essential.

(See Section 5 for the full continuity model.)

#### AXIS 3: PROTAGONIST MODEL
- **Fixed** — One protagonist across all books.
- **Fixed Core + Rotating** — Central protagonist with different co-protagonists per book.
- **Rotating** — Different protagonists, linked by setting/theme.
- **Generational** — Protagonists succeed across time, linked by family or legacy.
- **Ensemble** — Co-equal cast.
- **Anthological** — No recurring protagonist; linkage is thematic or world-based.
- **Institutional** — A place, role, or institution is the true protagonist. Characters serve the institution, which endures beyond any individual. (Salvage from Candidate A — addresses hospital dramas, police procedurals, starship crews, guild series where the institution is the series's real constant.)

#### AXIS 4: CHARACTER PERMANENCE
- **Static** — Characters reset to baseline.
- **Accreting** — Characters accumulate experiences; core remains stable.
- **Evolving** — Characters change permanently. Past events shape who they are.
- **Transformative** — Transformation IS the series.

#### AXIS 5: WORLD STATE
- **Renewable** — World resets between books.
- **Accreting** — World accumulates history; fundamental nature stable.
- **Evolving** — World changes meaningfully. Past events reshape the setting.
- **Revolutionary** — World undergoes fundamental transformation.

#### AXIS 6: RESOURCE MODEL
- **Renewable-dominant** — Series runs on resources that regenerate.
- **Finite-dominant** — Series runs on resources that don't regenerate.
- **Hybrid** — Both; balance shifts across the series.

#### AXIS 7: ESCALATION MODE
- **Variable** — No prescribed pattern.
- **Deepening** — Same territory, increasing complexity.
- **Widening** — Scope expands.
- **Ascending** — Stakes/scale increase.
- **Lateral** — Different angles, same scope.
- **Consequential** — Each book deals with previous book's consequences.
- **Revelatory** — Each book reveals hidden layers of the past.

(These are not mutually exclusive. The axis records the *primary* mode.)

### 3.3 Common Patterns

Seven patterns emerge from axis combinations. They are **descriptive, not prescriptive**.

**PATTERN A: RECURRING ADVENTURER** — Open-ended, Episodic/Progressive, Fixed protagonist, Static/Accreting, Renewable world, Renewable-dominant, Variable/Lateral.  
**Engine:** Protagonist + renewable incidents  
**Risk:** Protagonist exhaustion, repetitive sequels  
**Natural ending:** Protagonist retires/dies, or world stops generating viable incidents.

**PATTERN B: OVERARCHING SAGA** — Fixed/Bounded, Sequential, Fixed/Ensemble, Evolving/Transformative, Evolving/Revolutionary, Finite-dominant, Ascending/Consequential.  
**Engine:** The central question/mystery  
**Risk:** Premature climax, endless middle, mythology inflation, extension beyond natural ending  
**Natural ending:** Central question answered, war won, transformation complete.

**PATTERN C: RECURRING SETTING** — Open-ended, Episodic/Progressive, Rotating/Fixed Core + Rotating, Accreting/Evolving, Renewable/Accreting, Renewable-dominant, Deepening/Lateral.  
**Engine:** Setting + rotating cast  
**Risk:** Canon creep, status-quo paralysis, reader-promise drift  
**Natural ending:** Setting stops generating viable stories, or community changed beyond recognition.

**PATTERN D: EVOLUTION ARC** — Bounded/Approximate, Progressive/Sequential, Fixed, Evolving/Transformative, Any, Finite-dominant (growth IS the resource), Deepening/Consequential.  
**Engine:** Character growth vector  
**Risk:** Protagonist exhaustion, extension beyond natural ending  
**Natural ending:** Character's growth complete.

**PATTERN E: GENERATIONAL** — Bounded, Progressive, Generational, Evolving (within generations), Evolving, Hybrid, Consequential/Ascending.  
**Engine:** Family legacy + generational arcs  
**Risk:** Repetitive generational patterns, losing the throughline  
**Natural ending:** Family legacy resolved, or generational pattern broken.

**PATTERN F: HYBRID** — Any combination. Most real series are hybrids.  
**Engine:** Depends on configuration  
**Risk:** Components conflict (episodic surface demands reset, relationship arc demands progression)  
**Natural ending:** Depends on which component is dominant.

**PATTERN G: ANTHOLOGY / LINKED** — Open-ended/Bounded, Episodic, Anthological/Institutional, N/A per book, Renewable/Accreting, Renewable-dominant, Variable/Lateral.  
**Engine:** Thematic/world linkage  
**Risk:** Cohesion loss, reader-promise drift  
**Natural ending:** Theme exhausted, or world stops generating interesting stories.

### 3.4 Institutional Protagonist as a Legitimate Model

(Salvage from Candidate A) The institutional protagonist model — where a hospital, precinct, ship, school, or guild is the series's true constant — is not a subtype of ensemble. It has distinct structural properties:

- Characters serve the institution; the institution endures beyond any individual
- Cast rotation is natural, not forced (staff leave, new staff arrive)
- The institution's culture, reputation, and history become series-level canon
- The reader promise is tied to the institution's identity, not any character's
- Renewable engine: the institution generates new situations through its function

This model resolves the "hospital drama problem" — series where individual characters exhaust but the institution remains generative. It should be declared explicitly in the Contract when it applies, because it changes how protagonist exhaustion, cast rotation, and reader-promise questions are answered.

### 3.5 Phased Architecture Transitions

(Salvage from Candidate A — earned through TC-10 testing) Series may **deliberately transition** between architectures over their lifespan. This is not drift; it is conscious evolution.

Common transitions:
- Open-Ended → Hybrid (relationship/mythology arc emerges organically)
- Hybrid → Closed Saga (central arc becomes dominant)
- Single Protagonist → Generational (protagonist ages out)
- Shared World → Crossover Saga (cross-series arc introduced)
- Episodic → Serial (mythology accumulates)

**Rule:** Transitions require **deliberate reclassification** — never accidental drift. A transition triggers a Contract review (see Section 9) and a phase appendix recording what changed, why, and what safeguards apply to the new architecture.

## 4. NARRATIVE ECONOMY

### 4.1 Resource Categories

Five categories of narrative resource. Understanding which resources your series relies on, and which category each falls into, is the foundation of longevity planning.

#### GENERATORS — produce story capacity for future books
Examples: Unresolved character tensions, open world questions, character growth vectors, thematic depth, social dynamics, moral complexity, external situations that create new incidents.

Generators must be **maintained**. If resolved, they stop generating. Some are renewable (new mysteries arrive); some are finite (the overarching mystery can only be solved once).

#### CONSUMERS — spent by books
Examples: Resolved plot questions, dead characters, answered worldbuilding questions, closed character arcs, resolved relationship tensions, revealed secrets, spent thematic territory.

Consumption is what books *do*. The question is whether consumption is matched by generation.

#### PROTECTED — must not be spent too early
Examples: The central mystery/question, protagonist's defining wound, major relationship transformations, core world revelations, protagonist's identity revelation.

Protected resources have a **release schedule** — a sense of when they should be spent. If you spend a protected resource in Book 2, you need to know what will drive Books 3-7.

#### RENEWABLE — regenerate after use
Examples: New mysteries, new cases, new character introductions, new facets of established characters, new angles on themes, new consequences of past events, new combinations of existing elements.

#### FINITE — do not regenerate
Examples: The overarching mystery, character age/time, relationship firsts, world revelations, death, major character transformations, backstory revelations.

Finite resources must be **budgeted**. A series with finite resources and no renewable resources has a hard ceiling.

### 4.2 The Interest Diagnostic

A critical distinction: a renewable resource that generates *incidents* is not the same as one that generates *interest*.

New crimes arrive in a detective's jurisdiction every book — that's renewable. But if the detective's methods, personality, and moral framework have been fully explored, the new crimes generate incidents without generating interest.

**Diagnostic question:** Is the renewable resource the thing that makes the series interesting, or is it just the thing that generates incidents?

If the answer is "just incidents," the series's actual engine is something else — and that something else may be finite. Many series that appear renewable are running on a finite engine disguised as renewable.

### 4.3 Evolution That Creates Capacity

The ideal evolution generates new story capacity rather than merely spending existing resources:

- Character growth opens new relationship dynamics
- World revelation opens new territory
- Resolved mystery reveals deeper layer generating new questions
- Relationship transformation creates new tensions
- Consequence of past book creates new story material

This is the difference between a series that *ages* (spending resources) and one that *grows* (creating new resources through evolution).

### 4.4 Regenerative Assets

(Salvage from Candidate A — earned through independent critique) Some elements evolve *through consumption* — the act of resolving them creates new possibilities:

- Protagonist skill increases → new class of threats becomes viable
- Relationship deepens → new vulnerabilities emerge (betrayal, sacrifice, legacy stakes)
- World knowledge expands → deeper layers revealed
- Institutional position rises → greater responsibility, political intrigue
- Moral complexity accumulates → harder choices, no-win scenarios

Regenerative assets are the bridge between renewable and finite. They are consumed (the old version is gone) but the consumption generates something new. A series with strong regenerative assets can sustain finite-dominant architecture longer than expected.

## 5. CONTINUITY MODEL

### 5.1 Three Tiers

**TIER 1: EPISODIC (Low Dependency)**
Almost any volume can be read first.
- Each book self-contained; reading order flexible
- Character relationships may shift but don't require sequential reading
- World state largely stable
- Prior books enrich but aren't required

**Strengths:** Low reader barrier, high accessibility  
**Weaknesses:** Risk of stasis, repetition, status-quo paralysis  
**Best for:** Recurring adventurer, recurring setting, anthology, institutional

**TIER 2: PROGRESSIVE (Medium Dependency)**
Books resolve individually while relationships, world, and character arcs progress.
- Each book has self-contained plot
- Relationships, world state, character development advance
- Reading out of order loses context but doesn't break comprehension
- Prior books assumed but not required

**Strengths:** Combines accessibility with progression  
**Weaknesses:** Risk of canon creep, accidental dependency  
**Best for:** Hybrid series, recurring setting with evolving cast, evolution arc

**TIER 3: SEQUENTIAL (High Dependency)**
Books form a sequential larger narrative.
- Reading order essential
- Each book assumes reader knows prior events
- Individual books may not have self-contained plots

**Strengths:** Maximum momentum, deep investment  
**Weaknesses:** High reader barrier, hardest to sustain  
**Best for:** Overarching saga, generational

### 5.2 Continuity Drift

Series tend to drift toward higher dependency over time. This is natural — accumulated history creates context. But it has costs:
- Reader barrier increases
- Canon burden grows
- Freedom decreases

The framework asks the author to:
1. **Declare the intended tier** before Book 1
2. **Monitor drift** — is the series drifting higher than intended?
3. **Decide whether drift is acceptable** — some series benefit; others are damaged
4. **Take corrective action** if unacceptable

### 5.3 Drift Detection

(Salvage from Candidate A — practical, earned through TC-03 and TC-08) Candidate A's "Quarterly Architecture Audit" was calendar-based bureaucracy. Candidate C replaces it with a simpler principle:

**Review at natural milestones:** After each book, ask:
- Can a new reader still enter at this book?
- Has the series accumulated canon that prior books didn't require?
- Has the continuity tier shifted from what was declared?

If the answer to any of these is "yes" and the shift wasn't deliberate, it's drift. Decide: accept, correct, or reclassify with a Contract phase appendix.

## 6. ESCALATION

### 6.1 Against "Bigger Threat" Default

Ascending escalation (each book's threat is bigger) is one mode, not the default. It has a specific failure profile: sequel inflation, premature climax, exhaustion of scale.

### 6.2 Seven Escalation Modes

The framework recognises seven modes (see Axis 7). These are the **structural patterns** of how a series progresses across books:

- **Deepening** — Same situation, more emotional/psychological complexity
- **Lateral** — Different perspectives, same scope
- **Revelatory** — What seemed simple revealed as complex
- **Ascending** — Stakes/scale increase
- **Consequential** — Each book lives with what the previous book did
- **Widening** — Scope of the world expands
- **Variable** — No prescribed pattern

A series that only escalates by scale will run out of scale. A series that can escalate in multiple modes has more room.

### 6.3 Escalation Dimensions and Lenses

Within any escalation mode, the author can examine **what specifically escalates** through different dimensions or lenses. These are not additional modes — they are ways of seeing what deepens, widens, or progresses within the chosen mode:

- **Emotional lens:** The emotional stakes increase without external stakes changing
- **Moral lens:** The ethical complexity increases
- **Structural lens:** The relationship between books changes
- **Consequence lens:** Past choices return with weight
- **Relational lens:** Bonds are tested in new ways
- **Complexity lens:** More moving parts, less clarity
- **Thematic lens:** Core theme examined from new angles
- **Personal stakes lens:** What the protagonist risks/loses grows
- **Revelatory lens:** Deeper layers of the world are exposed
- **Irreversibility lens:** Changes become more permanent

These lenses help the author find *how* to escalate within a mode. For example, a series using Deepening mode might apply the emotional and moral lenses to find new depth. A series using Consequential mode might apply the relational and irreversibility lenses to track how consequences land.

The key principle: **modes are the structural pattern; lenses are what you look through to find the specific progression.** A series has one primary mode but may use multiple lenses within that mode.

### 6.4 The Deepen/Vary/Complicate/Progress Test

(Salvage from Candidate A — practical and earned through TC-05 testing) For every proposed sequel, ask:

> Does this sequel DEEPEN, VARY, COMPLICATE, or MEANINGFULLY PROGRESS the series — or merely ENLARGE it?

- **Deepen:** Reveals new layer of existing element
- **Vary:** Uses renewable engine in genuinely new way
- **Complicate:** Adds meaningful difficulty without just scaling
- **Progress:** Advances a committed arc

**Inflation signals:** "Bigger version of last threat," "same structure, higher stakes," "new villain who is just stronger," "more characters, same dynamics."

A book that only enlarges without deepening, varying, complicating, or progressing is running on inflation.

## 7. VARIATION PLANNING

### 7.1 The Variation Matrix

(Salvage from Candidate A — practical tool, earned through TC-05 testing) For each series, build a matrix of what repeats, what rotates, and what progresses:

| Element | Category | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| Primary location | SHOULD ROTATE | New location per book (or per arc) |
| Antagonist type | SHOULD ROTATE | New archetype per book |
| Core relationship | SHOULD PROGRESS | Deepen per book |
| Protagonist competence | SHOULD PROGRESS | Advance per book |
| Case/story structure | MAY REPEAT WITH VARIATION | Vary the type |
| Thematic focus | SHOULD ROTATE | New theme per book |
| Protagonist voice | SHOULD REPEAT | The thing readers return for |
| Setting atmosphere | SHOULD REPEAT | Part of the reader promise |

**Five categories:**
- **SHOULD REPEAT** — Recognisable pleasures readers return for. Every book.
- **MAY REPEAT WITH VARIATION** — Structures creating familiarity + freshness. Most books.
- **SHOULD ROTATE** — Elements cycled to prevent fatigue. Per book/arc.
- **SHOULD PROGRESS** — Elements that must advance. Per book.
- **MUST NEVER SIMPLY REPEAT** — Repetition cheapens earlier books. Never.

### 7.2 Familiarity Without Duplication

Test per book:
- Reader recognises the *kind* of story they're getting
- Reader encounters *new* specific execution
- At least one progression element advances
- No "first time" discovery repeated
- No resolved beat re-enacted

### 7.3 Simplicity Principle

The matrix should serve the series, not burden it. For simple series (3-4 books, single architecture), a minimal matrix (4-6 elements) is sufficient. For complex series (7+ books, hybrid architecture), the full matrix is warranted. The Contract (Section 8) specifies when the full matrix is needed.

## 8. LONGEVITY PRINCIPLES

### 8.1 The Reader Promise

Every series makes an implicit promise. Two components:
- **Experiential** — what it feels like to read (tone, pace, atmosphere)
- **Structural** — what the series delivers (mystery solved, adventure completed, character grown)

Reader-promise drift occurs when the series becomes something different. Unconscious drift alienates readers. Conscious evolution is a creative decision.

Questions:
1. What is the reader promise?
2. Can it be maintained across the planned books?
3. Is it designed to evolve? If so, how?
4. What would constitute a promise violation?

### 8.2 Generation-Consume Balance

A series is sustainable when each book generates at least as much story capacity as it consumes. Not quantitative — qualitative:
- Did this book's resolution create new material?
- Did character development open new dimensions?
- Did world revelation create new territory?
- Or did this book spend resources without generating?

Multiple consecutive deficit books indicate approaching exhaustion.

### 8.3 Protected Resource Pacing

Finite and protected resources must be paced:
- What must not be revealed/solved/transformed before a certain point?
- What happens if it is? What drives the remaining books?
- Is there enough protected resource for the planned length?

### 8.4 The Renewable Engine

Open-ended and long-running series need a renewable engine — a structural element generating new story material without consuming finite resources.

If a series has no renewable engine, it is finite by definition. Fine for sagas; fatal for open-ended series declared as such.

### 8.5 Knowing the Exit

Every series needs a **natural ending condition** — a compass, not a plan:
- A saga ends when the central question is answered
- An evolution arc ends when growth is complete
- A recurring adventurer ends when the protagonist retires or incidents exhaust
- A generational series ends when the family legacy is resolved

### 8.6 Architecture Change Conditions

Series architectures change. A progressive series becomes sequential. An open-ended series finds an ending. These changes are healthy when conscious and dangerous when accidental.

Define:
1. What would justify changing the architecture?
2. What signals would indicate a change is needed?
3. What changes are off the table?

### 8.7 Sunset and Successor Logic

(Salvage from Candidate A — earned through TC-07 testing) Open-ended series that don't plan an ending still need to know how they would end if they must:

**Sunset Protocol:** If the series must end unexpectedly (engine failure, creative exhaustion, market change), how does it conclude gracefully? What final arc shape? What handoff?

**Successor Series:** When a series reaches its natural ending but the world has more stories, a successor series is a designed handoff, not a drift into extension. It requires:
- A new Contract (not an extension of the old one)
- A clear distinction from the original (new protagonist, new phase, new engine)
- Preservation of what readers loved about the original (the reader promise carries forward in adapted form)
- Explicit recognition that the original series is complete

## 9. CANON BURDEN

### 9.1 The Canon Accumulation Problem

Every book adds to the canon — established facts the series must honour. As canon grows:
- Freedom decreases
- Reader barrier increases
- Author burden increases
- Contradiction risk increases

### 9.2 Canon Management Strategies

- **Closure** — Resolve and close threads rather than leaving them open
- **Compartmentalisation** — Keep elements self-contained within books
- **Controlled accumulation** — Manage what enters series-level canon
- **Reset moments** — Periodic shedding (time jump, cast change, new setting)
- **Recap architecture** — Build recap into narrative

### 9.3 Canon Creep Detection

(Replaces Candidate A's Canon Burden Index with qualitative assessment) Instead of a numerical index, ask three diagnostic questions at each review:

1. **Entry test:** Could a new reader start at the most recent book and follow what's happening?
2. **Author burden test:** Does the author need to check earlier books to avoid contradictions?
3. **Canon-to-story test:** Is the series's own history becoming a character, or is it serving the current story?

If any answer indicates the canon has grown beyond the intended tier, it's creep. Decide: accept, correct, or reclassify.

### 9.4 Contradiction Detection

(Salvage from Candidate A — lightweight version, earned through testing) Before Contract finalisation, check for incompatible combinations:

| # | Contradiction | Why It Fails |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Open-ended + strong finite central arc | Arc consumes engine; no renewal post-resolution |
| 2 | Low dependency + major cliffhangers | Violates "read anywhere" promise |
| 3 | Evolving protagonist + premise requiring status quo | Growth breaks recurring function |
| 4 | Open-ended + rapidly consumable core mystery | Mystery consumed; no engine |
| 5 | Standalone books + later volumes requiring prior knowledge | Misclassified dependency |
| 6 | Single protagonist + real-time ageing + unlimited + no succession | Protagonist ages out of role |
| 7 | High-dependency saga + no planned escalation | Middle volumes stagnate |
| 8 | Rotating protagonists + central emotional arc stranded on departed protagonist | Arc has no resolution path |
| 9 | Hybrid + continuing arc overwhelms book closure | Becomes de facto serial |
| 10 | Anthology + cross-book canon required | Misclassified type |
| 11 | Generational + no generational engine defined | Succession has no story function |

**Severity levels:**
- **Hard incompatibility** — Contract invalid; redesign required
- **Risk** — Must be acknowledged with mitigation plan
- **Heuristic warning** — Documented; monitor

The distinction matters: some contradictions are structural (open-ended + finite central arc = the engine dies), while others are risks that can be managed with awareness (hybrid arc dominance can be corrected with book-closure enforcement).

## 10. MULTI-LEVEL SERIES, SUB-SERIES, AND SPIN-OFFS

(Resolves Candidate B gap — identified during testing as "single-unit assumption")

### 10.1 The Problem

The framework treats a series as a single unit. But real series often have sub-series, spin-offs, trilogies within larger series, or multi-level structures (e.g., a shared world with multiple sub-series, each following different characters).

### 10.2 Multi-Level Contract Architecture

For multi-level series, use a **master contract + sub-contracts**:

- **Master Contract:** Covers the shared-world or series-level architecture (world state, canon rules, reading-order policy, cross-series escalation, overarching questions)
- **Sub-Contracts:** One per sub-series or spin-off, covering the sub-series's own architecture (protagonist, closure model, escalation, failure mode exposure)

Each sub-contract inherits from the master contract but adds its own structural decisions. Changes to the master contract propagate; changes to a sub-contract are local.

### 10.3 When to Use Multi-Level

Use multi-level contracts when:
- A shared world has multiple protagonist arcs running in parallel
- A sub-series splits off from a main series with a different architecture
- A trilogy within a larger open-ended series has a different closure model

Don't use multi-level when the series is genuinely single-unit. Over-engineering multi-level contracts for a simple series creates bureaucracy.

## 11. TRANSITIONS BETWEEN PROTAGONIST/ARCHITECTURE MODELS

(Resolves Candidate B gap — identified during testing as "the transition itself isn't modelled")

### 11.1 The Problem

Series change protagonists or architecture models mid-life. A fixed protagonist becomes generational. An episodic series becomes sequential. The framework can describe the before and after states, but not the transition itself.

### 11.2 Transition Protocol

When a series transitions between models, use a **phase appendix** to the Contract:

1. **Record the original architecture** (what it was)
2. **Record the new architecture** (what it's becoming)
3. **Record the transition trigger** (what event/condition necessitates the change)
4. **Record the reader-promise impact** (does the reader experience change? How?)
5. **Record what safeguards still apply** and which need to be replaced
6. **Record newly introduced risks** specific to the new architecture
7. **Record Andrew's approval** of the transition

### 11.3 Transition Patterns

Common transitions and their key risks:

| Transition | Key Risk | What Must Be Preserved |
|---|---|---|
| Single → Generational | Reader attachment to original protagonist | Reader promise (what readers loved about the series) |
| Episodic → Sequential | Reader barrier increase | Entry points (or accept the barrier) |
| Open-ended → Bounded | Premature ending | Remaining story capacity (enough for the new length) |
| Hybrid → Saga | Book closure lost | Reader satisfaction per book (or accept serialised form) |
| Rotating → Fixed | Other protagonists abandoned | World continuity (the fixed protagonist inherits the world) |

### 11.4 Non-Linear Chronology

(Resolves Candidate B gap — identified during TC-10 testing)

Series with non-linear timelines (backward, interleaved, non-chronological) need axis adaptation:

- **Character Permanence:** "Evolution" can mean revealing earlier states, not just forward change
- **World State:** "Evolving" can mean devolving (going backward reveals less developed world)
- **Escalation:** "Revelatory" mode (added to Candidate B during testing) specifically handles backward-revelation structures
- **Continuity:** May be progressive in revelation order even if not in chronological order

The framework supports non-linear timelines with these adaptations. Authors should note in the Contract when the timeline is non-linear and which axes have been adapted.

## 12. THROUGHLINE AND COHESION MANAGEMENT

(Resolves Candidate B gap — identified during testing of rotating, generational, and shared-world series)

### 12.1 The Throughline Problem

For rotating-protagonist, generational, and shared-world series, the reader's connection is through the throughline (family legacy, shared world, thematic identity), not any individual book's protagonist. The framework identifies this but doesn't guide how to maintain it.

### 12.2 Throughline Anchoring

A throughline needs **anchors** — recurring elements that appear across books, creating series identity:

- **World anchors:** A location, institution, or setting element that appears in every book
- **Character anchors:** A recurring character (not the protagonist) who connects books — a mentor, a bartender, a historian, a child who grows across books
- **Thematic anchors:** A question or theme examined from different angles in each book
- **Artifact anchors:** A physical object, document, or tradition that recurs
- **Institutional anchors:** For institutional-protagonist series, the institution itself is the anchor

### 12.3 Cohesion Management

For each series with rotating/generational/shared-world architecture, ask:
1. What is the throughline?
2. What are its anchors?
3. Is each anchor strong enough to create series identity without requiring sequential reading?
4. If an anchor weakens (a recurring character leaves, a setting changes), what replaces it?

A series that loses its throughline suffers **cohesion loss** — the books stop feeling like a series (see Failure Mode 13).

## 13. ANTHOLOGY HANDLING

(Resolves Candidate B gap — identified during TC-07 testing: narrative economy provides limited value for pure anthologies)

### 13.1 The Anthology Distinction

Pure anthologies (no shared characters, no shared world, only thematic linkage) have almost no series-level narrative economy. Each book is self-contained. The framework's resource model provides little insight.

### 13.2 What the Framework Offers Anthologies

For pure anthologies, the framework's value is in:
- **Reader-promise analysis** — what does the thematic linkage promise?
- **Cohesion analysis** — is the linking element specific enough to create series identity?
- **Failure mode exposure** — cohesion loss is the primary risk

The narrative economy, continuity model, escalation modes, and canon burden sections are not applicable to pure anthologies. Skip them. Do not force economic machinery where it adds no value.

### 13.3 Setting Anthologies

Setting anthologies (shared world, different characters, different eras) sit between pure anthologies and shared-world series. The world is the throughline. Apply the throughline/cohesion management section (12) but skip the narrative economy unless the world accumulates canon across books.

## 14. APPLYING THE FRAMEWORK

### 14.1 Pre-Production Sequence

1. **Declare the structural axes** (Section 3.2)
2. **Identify the narrative economy** (Section 4) — generators, consumers, protected, renewable, finite, regenerative
3. **Apply the Interest Diagnostic** (Section 4.2) — is the renewable engine generating interest or just incidents?
4. **Declare the continuity tier** (Section 5)
5. **Choose the escalation mode(s)** (Section 6)
6. **Build the variation matrix** (Section 7) — if applicable (skip for pure anthologies)
7. **Articulate the reader promise** (Section 8.1)
8. **Define the natural ending condition** (Section 8.5)
9. **Define sunset/successor logic** (Section 8.7) — if open-ended
10. **Define architecture change conditions** (Section 8.6)
11. **Check for contradictions** (Section 9.4)
12. **Address multi-level/transition/throughline needs** (Sections 10-12) — if applicable
13. **Complete the Series Longevity Contract** (separate document)
14. **Review failure mode exposure** (separate document)

### 14.2 Review Points

- After Book 1 (did the architecture hold?)
- Before each subsequent book (is the series on track?)
- When a structural problem is detected
- When a creative opportunity conflicts with the architecture
- At each natural milestone (after every 3-4 books, or per series arc)

### 14.3 What This Framework Does Not Do

- It does not evaluate story quality
- It does not predict commercial success
- It does not prescribe creative choices
- It does not replace the author's judgement
- It does not guarantee longevity

It makes structural decisions visible so they can be made consciously.

## 15. LIMITATIONS

1. **Unvalidated** — Based on storycraft reasoning, not empirical study.
2. **Western/novel-centric** — Assumes multi-book prose fiction. Other forms may need adaptation.
3. **Author-facing** — Designed for authors and architects, not editors, publishers, or marketers.
4. **No quality guarantee** — Perfect architecture can still fail through poor execution.
5. **Self-assessment dependency** — Only as good as the author's honest self-assessment. The Interest Diagnostic (4.2) is designed to surface blind spots but cannot force honest assessment.
6. **Non-linear timelines** — Axes assume forward progression. Non-linear series need axis adaptation (Section 11.4).
7. **Anthology limitation** — Narrative economy provides limited value for pure anthologies (Section 13).
8. **Single-author assumption** — Does not address collaborative series with multiple authors or shared-universe governance.
9. **Recovery limitation** — Can diagnose problems and suggest forward-looking strategies, but cannot undo past damage.
10. **Multi-level complexity** — Multi-level contracts add overhead. Use only when genuinely needed.

## APPENDIX: GLOSSARY

**Narrative Economy** — The system of resources that generate, consume, and preserve story capacity.
**Generator** — A resource that produces story capacity.
**Consumer** — A resource spent by books.
**Protected Resource** — A resource that must not be spent too early.
**Renewable Resource** — A resource that regenerates after use.
**Finite Resource** — A resource that does not regenerate.
**Regenerative Asset** — An element that evolves through consumption, generating new possibilities. (Salvage from Candidate A)
**Reader Promise** — The implicit contract between series and reader.
**Canon Burden** — Accumulated continuity the series must honour.
**Continuity Drift** — The tendency of series to accumulate dependency over time.
**Natural Ending Condition** — A statement of what would make the series complete.
**Architecture Change Condition** — A predefined trigger for revisiting the architecture.
**Generation-Consume Balance** — Whether a series creates capacity as fast as it spends it.
**Renewable Engine** — A structural element generating new story material without consuming finite resources.
**Canon Creep** — Gradual accumulation of canon in a series designed to be low-dependency.
**Extension Territory** — A series that has passed its natural ending but continues.
**Interest Diagnostic** — The distinction between resources generating incidents vs. resources generating interest.
**Institutional Protagonist** — A place, role, or institution as the series's true constant. (Salvage from Candidate A)
**Variation Matrix** — Classification of what repeats, rotates, and progresses across books. (Salvage from Candidate A)
**Deepen/Vary/Complicate/Progress Test** — Test for whether a sequel develops or merely enlarges. (Salvage from Candidate A)
**Sunset Protocol** — Graceful ending plan for open-ended series that must end. (Salvage from Candidate A)
**Successor Series** — A designed handoff to a new series when the original is complete. (Salvage from Candidate A)
**Phase Appendix** — Contract addendum recording a deliberate architecture transition. (Salvage from Candidate A)
**Throughline Anchor** — A recurring element creating series identity across rotating/generational/shared-world books.
