Mara knelt beside Councillor Saye’s car and put two fingers into the shallow spread beneath its chassis. The water was cold enough to numb the tips at once. She rubbed a bead between finger and thumb, then touched it to her tongue.

Fresh water.

Not rainwater. Rain had blown across the vehicle deck in silver sheets, but the centre lane sat beneath the enclosed roof and the car’s underside was dry above the puddle. She tasted again, against habit and common sense. No salt. No oil. No trace of the estuary.

The ferry rolled beneath a gust. Somewhere above, metal flexed with a deep, brief groan.

‘What are you doing down there?’ Councillor Saye demanded.

His voice carried from the driver’s side window, sharp with the authority he used at council meetings. He stood in the narrow gap between the car and the painted safety rail, one hand pressed to the roof. His grey document case was wedged against his shin. The case had two brass catches and no visible lock, though Mara had seen him check it twice before they left Bellwether.

‘Finding water where it shouldn’t be.’

‘There’s water everywhere. The storm’s been throwing it over the deck.’

‘Not here.’

He looked down at the puddle as if it were an accusation he might answer by refusing to recognise it. The ferry’s centre lane was enclosed on three sides and roofed over. Spray could reach the outer vehicles, perhaps, but not the space beneath his sedan.

Nessa stood beside the passenger lounge door, red canvas backpack held against her ribs. Seventeen, Mara guessed, though she had seen the girl’s age on the booking sheet earlier. Nessa’s hair was wet at the ends. Her paper ticket was pinched between two fingers, its edge softened from rain.

Saye pointed at it.

‘The ticket is the issue here. A stamped 23:40 ticket, presented after departure, from a passenger who claims she was waiting on the wharf. We need to establish whether she boarded this ferry lawfully before we start inventing problems under my car.’

‘We haven’t started inventing anything,’ Mara said.

‘The crossing is already outside schedule.’

The ferry clock over the lounge entrance showed 11:48. Its second hand jerked forward, paused, then jerked again. Mara checked her wristwatch.

11:55.

Seven minutes slow, as always. The old clock had been logged for repair twice and ignored twice. Official times went by actual time, not the ferry’s stubborn face, but passengers used the clock when they wanted to make a point.

Saye lifted his chin towards Nessa. ‘Ask her where she came from. Ask her who stamped that ticket.’

Nessa’s eyes remained on Mara. They were pale and steady, with a dark crescent of sleeplessness beneath each one.

‘Eli sent me a message tonight.’

The name struck harder than the wind against the hull. Mara kept her hand in the puddle, though the water had begun to run cold through her glove cuff.

Eli.

For eleven years his name had lived in Bellwether as a conclusion. Marina fire. Missing man. Convenient absence. People had spoken around the gaps until the gaps became evidence. Mara had said nothing when they blamed him. At the hearing, she had watched her mother’s hands twist a paper handkerchief into threads and had kept her own mouth shut.

Saye gave a short, disbelieving laugh. ‘That is not relevant to the ticket.’

‘You know him?’ Mara asked.

Nessa’s fingers closed over the paper. ‘I know who he is.’

‘What did he say?’

‘You should ask him.’

The ferry dipped. The puddle slid towards the drain in a thin, clear sheet. Mara followed it with her eyes. It vanished beneath Saye’s car, not towards the scupper on the outer edge but inward, under the locked centre lane where no deck water should have been waiting.

From the wheelhouse above came the muted thrum of the engine telegraph. Tom Arlen stayed at the controls while under way. He would hear the change in pitch, but he would not leave the wheelhouse to investigate it. Tom believed a vessel was safest when every person kept to the job assigned to them.

Saye crouched with difficulty, his document case scraping the deck. ‘Councillor or not, I’m not permitting a child to wander around making claims about a dead—’

‘Don’t.’

Mara did not raise her voice. She had to stop the word before it finished, and the effort showed in the tightness of her jaw.

Saye looked from her to Nessa. ‘You’re emotionally involved.’

‘Yes.’

The answer unsettled him more than anger would have. He rose, dragging the case upright against his leg. ‘The ticket still needs to be checked.’

A small panel beside the maintenance stair shuddered. Three short beeps sounded in the deck’s enclosed space, bright and mechanical. The alarm light changed from green to red.

Mara turned towards the panel.

The maintenance stair ran up from the vehicle deck to the service passage beneath the wheelhouse. Its door was locked during crossings, and the alarm recorded every opening with an actual timestamp. The system had been installed after a deckhand fell between shifts and spent twenty minutes trapped in the lower bilge access. No one opened that door casually.

Saye’s face had gone still. ‘That could be a fault.’

‘Could be.’

‘You were going to question the girl.’

‘In a minute.’

Nessa stepped away from the lounge door. ‘Is someone in there?’

Mara looked at the girl’s backpack, then at the stair panel. The red indicator held steady. She could ask Nessa what Eli had sent, where the message had come from, why she had waited eleven years to carry it across the estuary. She could ask Saye why a man who insisted on procedure had brought a locked case into a storm and why his car now stood over a pool of inland water.

But the alarm had not sounded for questions.

She wiped her wet fingers on her work trousers and stood. The deck tilted beneath her boots, then settled. Saye reached for her elbow.

‘Mara, wait.’

She removed his hand.

‘Stay with Nessa. Don’t open the case. Don’t touch the car.’

‘I’m not taking instructions from—’

‘Then take them as advice.’

She crossed to the stair. With each step, the ferry’s engine became more distinct through the deck plates, a steady vibration pressing into her soles. The red alarm light painted the door’s metal handle the colour of fresh blood, though the thought arrived and went without gathering weight.

Her hand found the brass compass in her pocket. Eli’s compass, scratched along its lid, warmed by the heat of her body. She had carried it since the marina fire because throwing it away would have been easier than admitting she still wanted something of his near her.

The compass turned in Mara’s pocket.

Not a swing north, not the stubborn inland pull it had shown for years. The needle—or whatever moved inside the brass casing—rotated once, stopped, then rotated again. It provided no direction and no answer.

Behind her, Saye said, ‘Mara.’

Nessa said nothing.

Mara took the key ring from her belt. The alarm panel continued its red, patient glow. She selected the maintenance key by touch, fitted it into the lock, and turned. The mechanism resisted, then gave with a heavy click.

She gripped the handle and opened the maintenance-stair door.
