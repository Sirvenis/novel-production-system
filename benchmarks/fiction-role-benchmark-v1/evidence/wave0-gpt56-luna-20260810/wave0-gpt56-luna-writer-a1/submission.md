Mara knelt beside Councillor Saye’s car and touched the water with two fingers.

It ran in a narrow line from beneath the vehicle’s rear axle, gathering in the shallow grooves between the metal plates. Rain hammered the ferry’s enclosed centre lane overhead, but none of it should have been here. The water was clear enough to show the painted deck beneath it.

She lifted her fingers to her mouth.

Fresh.

Not brackish, not the oily tang of the estuary caught on a tyre. It tasted of rusted pipes and rainwater left in a tank.

Mara wiped her hand on her coveralls and looked beneath the car. The undercarriage was black with road grime. No split hose. No burst radiator. The water was coming from farther inboard, where Saye’s locked grey case sat on the passenger-side floor behind the front seat.

“You can’t hold the crossing for this,” Saye said.

He stood on the other side of the car, one hand braced against the roof while the ferry rolled down into the trough of a wave. At sixty-two, he had the compact, polished look of a man who had spent his life in rooms where other people waited for him to finish speaking. His raincoat was buttoned to the throat. His hair had not moved.

Mara looked at the grey case.

“I’m not holding anything.”

“You’re making an incident of a wet deck.”

“Fresh water under an enclosed vehicle lane is an incident.”

“The pump system could have backed up.”

“The pump system is on the other side of the bulkhead.”

Saye’s eyes shifted to the passenger lounge. “Then ask the girl.”

Nessa Quill sat on the bench by the lounge doors, red canvas backpack between her boots. She was seventeen, perhaps, though the hard set of her mouth made her seem older. Her paper ticket rested on her knee, the ink blurred at one corner but the stamp plain enough: 23:40.

“That ticket is the main issue,” Saye said. “She boarded after the cut-off. I saw her come through the gate.”

“You saw her ticket.”

“I saw what it said. It was stamped 23:40.”

Mara rose, steadying herself against the car as the ferry shuddered. The vehicle deck lights buzzed overhead. Salt spray flashed white beyond the side rails, then vanished into the dark. Under the centre lane, the fresh water continued to move in a thin, purposeful thread.

“Nessa,” Mara called.

The girl looked over.

“Where did you get the ticket?”

“Bellwether office.”

“Before eleven-forty?”

Nessa glanced at Saye. “They gave it to me.”

“That isn’t what I asked.”

Saye stepped closer. “You need to record that she presented a late ticket. If she was not booked on this sailing, she should not be here.”

“She’s here now.”

“That does not make it regular.”

Mara had seen the same look in council meetings, back when Eli’s name was still spoken without anyone lowering their voice. Saye had stood behind the marina fire report and said the town needed a clear account. He had not accused Eli outright. He had simply repeated the questions people were asking until the questions became a verdict.

The ferry climbed the next wave. Somewhere above them, the wheelhouse engine telegraph clacked. Tom would be watching the channel lights through the rain, hands fixed on the controls. He never left the wheelhouse under way. Mara could picture him there in the red instrument glow, jaw tucked into his collar, refusing to look down at the deck.

Nessa folded her ticket once, along the existing crease.

“Why are you travelling alone?” Mara asked.

The girl’s fingers tightened on the paper. “I’m going to the mainland.”

“That much is clear.”

“I have family there.”

“Name?”

Saye gave a short, impatient laugh. “This is exactly the problem. We’re wasting time on a passenger who shouldn’t have boarded while there’s a secured case leaking all over my vehicle.”

Mara crouched again and pressed her palm to the deck. Cold water spread beneath it. The metal trembled with the vibration of the engines.

“What’s in the case?”

“That is private.”

“Then it can stay private while you tell me why it’s leaking.”

“It isn’t leaking.”

She pointed to the stream.

“That water is not from my case.”

“Then you won’t mind if I look.”

Saye’s expression closed. “You have no authority to open it.”

Mara stood. In the pocket of her coveralls, Eli’s brass compass pressed against her hip. The lid had rubbed smooth over the years, the hinge loose enough to click when she walked. She had carried it since the marina fire because leaving it behind had felt too much like agreeing with everyone else.

Nessa was watching her now, not Saye.

“Did you put the water there?” Mara asked.

“No.”

“Did you see it before I came down?”

Nessa shook her head.

Saye turned on her. “You’ve been near this car since boarding.”

“I haven’t.”

“Your backpack was beside it.”

“On the other side of the deck.”

Mara watched the girl’s face. Nessa did not look frightened. She looked tired, and alert in the way of someone waiting for a door to open or a person to return.

Then she said, exactly, “Eli sent me a message tonight.”

The engines seemed to deepen beneath Mara’s feet.

Saye stopped talking.

Mara heard the rain against the roof, the loose rattle of something in a recessed panel, the faint clink of Nessa’s ticket against its plastic sleeve. Eli had been gone eleven years. There had been no message, no confirmed sighting, no body. Only the marina fire, the statements, the town’s appetite for a name that could be pinned to the blame.

“What message?” Mara asked.

Nessa’s gaze stayed on hers. “I’ll show you.”

“Now.”

Mara pulled her wristwatch into the light. The hands stood at 11:55.

Across the deck, the ferry clock above the lounge doors read 11:48.

For a moment she thought the second hand had stalled. It ticked forward, thin and precise.

The clock was seven minutes slow. She had reported it twice that week. Tom had said the replacement battery was on order.

Saye followed her gaze. “What?”

“Nothing.”

The word came out too quickly. Mara lowered her arm.

A sharp electronic beep sounded from the passage beside the vehicle deck. Then another. Then a third.

Three short beeps.

The maintenance alarm panel sat behind a wire guard near the locked stair door. Its red indicator had lit.

Mara turned towards it.

Saye caught her sleeve. “The passenger first.”

“The alarm records openings.”

“No one is in there.”

“Then we’ll confirm it.”

“You can’t leave this.”

She looked at the water, at the grey case, at Nessa’s red backpack. Three problems, none of them waiting politely for their turn. The alarm had a log. If the stair had opened, it would give her an actual time, not the ferry clock’s version of the night. If something had entered or left through there, questioning Nessa could wait.

Nessa rose from the bench.

“Stay there,” Mara said.

“I didn’t—”

“Stay with Councillor Saye.”

Saye’s hand fell away from Mara’s sleeve. “You’re making a serious mistake.”

She had made one before, though at the time it had looked like silence.

Mara crossed the deck. The ferry lurched, and the soles of her boots skidded through the fresh water. It was colder than the rainwater in the washroom tanks, cold enough to numb the skin through the leather.

At the alarm panel, she lifted the wire guard. The display showed a red line of digits, but the reflection from the deck lights broke it apart. She leaned closer.

23:53:17.

Actual time, according to the panel.

Seven minutes before her watch. The opening had been logged two minutes earlier than now.

Mara looked back.

Saye stood beside his car with both hands visible, as if anticipating an accusation. Nessa had not moved. Her backpack hung from one shoulder, the red canvas darkened by spray near the bottom. Between them, the thin freshwater stream slipped towards a drain.

In Mara’s pocket, the brass compass turned.

She felt the movement against her hip: a small, insistent drag, then a second shift as the ferry rolled. When she took it out, the needle trembled beneath its scratched glass. It swung inland, then away from inland, settling nowhere. The compass gave her no direction and no answer.

Mara closed her hand around it.

The maintenance-stair door was painted the same grey as the bulkhead. Its handle was cold. She placed her fingers around it, listening to the engines, the rain, and the water moving where it should not.

Then she opened the door.
