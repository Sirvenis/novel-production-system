Mara knelt beside Councillor Saye’s car and put two fingers into the shallow runnel beneath its front axle.

The water was cold enough to numb the pads of her fingers. It carried no diesel sheen, no grit of the river, no bitter tang of rust from the deck plates. She touched her wet fingers to her tongue.

Fresh.

She spat into the drain slot and wiped her mouth on the back of her glove.

Rainwater could not get here. The ferry’s vehicle deck was enclosed along the centre lane, steel roof above, side doors dogged shut for the crossing. Salt water blew in around the ramps sometimes, misting the deck and leaving white marks when it dried. Fresh water, gathered in a clean thread beneath a locked car, had no place in the list of ordinary faults.

“Is that necessary?” Councillor Hugh Saye asked.

He stood on the other side of his car, one hand clamped around the handle of his grey document case. His raincoat was buttoned to the throat. The case hung beside his knee, clean and square-edged despite the wet deck.

Mara rose, keeping her eyes on the water. “You’ve got a leak.”

“The car is not the concern.”

“It’s coming from under the car.”

“The girl’s ticket is the concern.” Saye’s voice carried across the vehicle deck, sharp enough to turn heads in the passenger lounge beyond the glass partition. “She presented a ticket stamped 23:40, after the manifest had closed. No name, no guardian, no booking.”

Nessa Quill stood near the centre-lane barrier with her red canvas backpack hugged to her chest. Seventeen, perhaps. Mara had seen her at the terminal, alone under the sodium lights while the wind worried at the ticket booth’s awning. The paper ticket was still in Nessa’s hand, softened at the corners but legible.

23:40.

The ferry clock above the lounge door read 11:48. Mara glanced at her wristwatch.

11:55.

Seven minutes slow. The old clock had been like that for years, a defect everyone knew and no one had bothered to correct because the crossing schedule ran on the wheelhouse instruments and the terminal clocks. Official incident times were another matter. Actual time. Tom had drilled that into her after a grounding near Pelican Point: write what the watch says, not what the ferry says.

Saye lifted his chin towards Nessa. “She shouldn’t be aboard.”

“She has a ticket.”

“Stamped at the departure time.”

“Then the booth clerk stamped it.”

“The booth clerk is at home, Councillor.”

Mara looked at him. “So you know who stamped it?”

His mouth tightened. “That is not what I said.”

The ferry rolled gently beneath them. Somewhere above, the hull gave a long, low groan as it took the pressure of the tide. Rain struck the roof in hard bursts, then softened. Through the sealed side windows, the estuary was only blackness and broken reflections from the deck lights.

Nessa’s eyes were on Mara. Her face had the pale, fixed look of someone trying not to shiver. Her dark hair was wet at the ends, and one white earbud cord disappeared into the collar of her jacket.

Saye shifted the document case from one hand to the other. “We need to establish how she got on. If she boarded without authorisation, we turn around.”

“You want to turn a loaded ferry around in mid-channel because a seventeen-year-old has a paper ticket?”

“I want the captain informed.”

“Tom is informed. He saw her board.”

“Then he can explain why he allowed it.”

Tom did not leave the wheelhouse while the vessel was under way. Mara could picture him there: shoulders hunched beneath the instrument panel’s green glow, one boot angled against the console, eyes moving between the channel markers and the radar. He would have heard the raised voices through the intercom speaker. He would not come down to settle an argument.

The water beneath Saye’s car gathered another bead and slid towards the drain.

Mara took a rag from her tool pouch and pressed it into the wet line. The cloth darkened immediately. No oily rainbow. No smell of coolant. She squeezed it over her palm and watched the drops fall.

“Ask her,” Saye said. “She’s the reason we’re losing time.”

“We’re not losing time.”

“Her ticket says otherwise.”

Nessa looked at the ticket as though it might have changed while they spoke. “It was on the counter.”

“That does not answer the question.”

Mara gave Saye a hard look. “You asked her why she’s aboard. Not why the clock is wrong.”

“The ticket is stamped 23:40.”

“Actual time was 23:40 when the ferry left. The clock is slow.”

“It wasn’t slow at the terminal.”

“It’s been slow since Tuesday.”

Saye’s gaze moved to the clock and back. “That is convenient.”

Mara heard the accusation beneath the word. She had heard versions of it for eleven years: convenient, careless, evasive. The town had used those words on Eli until they hardened into a verdict. She had stood behind the marina office window while people spoke about her brother and had said nothing that might have cost her a job, a friend, a clean place in town.

Nessa lowered the backpack to the deck. The bag landed with a wet, heavy thump.

Saye turned towards her. “Who bought the ticket?”

Nessa did not answer.

“Who told you to come to Bellwether?”

Her fingers tightened around the paper.

Mara saw the movement and the tiny crease it made across the ticket’s stamp. She knew that kind of pressure: the body holding still while the mind ran somewhere else.

Nessa looked at Mara. “Eli sent me a message tonight.”

The words sat between them, plain and impossible.

Saye’s expression changed first. Not surprise. Recognition, quickly covered.

Mara felt the deck tilt under one knee.

For a moment she saw the marina fire as it had been in the photographs: orange light behind the corrugated sheds, a hose lying useless in the mud, Eli’s face caught in a camera flash as police led him away. She had been twenty-seven and furious with him already. He had missed their mother’s surgery. He had borrowed money. He had disappeared before the smoke cleared.

Eleven years without a message.

Mara looked at Nessa’s phone. The screen was dark. “Show me.”

Saye stepped between them, his grey case brushing his leg. “This is exactly why we need to establish her status before—”

The maintenance alarm sounded.

Three short beeps came from the bulkhead beside the locked stair door.

Beep. Beep. Beep.

The noise was small, almost polite. It stopped the arguments more effectively than a shout.

Mara turned.

The maintenance stair was set into the rear bulkhead, behind a yellow-painted steel door with a wired-glass panel at eye level. Beyond it, the stair led up to the machinery spaces and the wheelhouse access landing. The door carried a red warning placard and a black alarm sensor above the latch.

The alarm panel showed no fault light from where she stood.

Saye said, “What was that?”

“Stair alarm.”

“Why would it sound?”

“Because the door opened.”

“Then check it.”

Mara looked at Nessa. The girl had gone rigid, one hand on the red backpack strap. The phone remained hidden against her jacket. Saye’s document case was pressed close to his thigh, his knuckles pale around the handle.

Mara could question Nessa now. She could take the phone, demand the message, ask what Eli had written and how she knew his name. Every instinct she had trained into herself told her to secure the information before it scattered.

The alarm had recorded an opening. Actual timestamp, not ferry-clock time. If someone had gone through that door, the first fact was not what they claimed. It was when.

She reached into her pocket for the brass compass.

The casing was warm from her body. Eli had given it to her when they were children, after finding it in a tackle box at the old marina. Since the fire, the needle had pointed inland instead of north. She had carried it anyway, through jobs, apartments, court interviews and the years when people stopped saying Eli’s name.

Now it turned beneath the glass.

Not north. Not inland. It swung clockwise, shuddered, and settled against the rim. When Mara shifted her hand, it moved again, circling without finding a bearing.

No direction. No answer.

She closed her fingers around it and put it back in her pocket.

“Stay here,” she told Nessa.

Saye made a disbelieving sound. “You’re leaving her?”

“I’m checking the alarm.”

“I’ll come with you.”

“No.”

He stared at her.

Mara pulled the rag from beneath the car and dropped it into her tool pouch. “You stay with the passenger. If the ferry rolls, keep away from the deck edge.”

“It’s a locked stair.”

“Then it should be empty.”

She crossed the lane before either of them could object. The deck vibrated through the soles of her boots. Behind her, Nessa said nothing. Saye began to speak, but Mara did not catch the words over the engine’s rising thrum and the rain beating on the steel roof.

At the door, she checked the alarm panel. The recorded light blinked once, then went dark.

She put her hand on the latch.

The metal was wet.

Mara opened the maintenance-stair door.
